Rainwater Harvesting in Indian Cities

Rainwater harvesting is the practice of collecting rain where it falls and
storing it for later use instead of allowing it to run off into drains. In
crowded Indian cities the rooftops of houses, schools and offices form a
surprisingly large catchment area, and even a single monsoon season can
deliver tens of thousands of litres from an ordinary terrace. A basic system
needs only four parts: a clean catchment surface, a gutter and downpipe, a
first flush device that throws away the dirty opening minutes of a storm, and
a storage tank or a recharge pit that returns the water to the ground.

The first flush device matters more than beginners expect. Dust, bird
droppings and leaves collect on a roof during the dry weeks before the rains,
and the first few minutes of runoff carry all of it. Diverting roughly the
first half millimetre of rainfall protects the quality of everything stored
afterwards, and it costs almost nothing to build from a length of pipe and a
valve.

Storage and recharge serve different purposes. A tank keeps water available
for washing, gardening and, after filtration, for drinking. A recharge pit
instead pushes water back into the aquifer, where it raises the water table
that neighbourhood borewells depend on. Cities such as Chennai made recharge
structures compulsory for new buildings after the water crisis of 2001, and
studies of the years that followed recorded a measurable recovery in
groundwater levels across several wards.

The economics are usually favourable. A rooftop system for a small house can
be built for the price of a few months of tanker deliveries, and it pays for
itself within two or three monsoons in any locality where piped supply is
unreliable. Maintenance is modest: clearing the gutters before the rains,
flushing the filter media once a season, and checking the tank for algae.

None of this replaces a municipal water supply. Rainfall in most of India
arrives in a short, violent burst, so a household that depends only on stored
rain will run dry long before the next monsoon. Harvesting is best understood
as a buffer that reduces demand on the piped network at the exact moment the
network is under greatest stress, and as a way of putting water back under
the city rather than watching it disappear down a storm drain.
